# Physical activity research pack

Status key: **Opened** = I read the official page or abstract. **Secondary** = figure taken from a search excerpt or press coverage. Verify secondary figures before printing.

## 2. Headline facts

**Fact 1 (steps and longevity)**
- Print: "In a study of US adults, those who averaged 8,000 steps a day had about half the risk of death over roughly 10 years compared with those who averaged 4,000."
- Stat: 8,000 steps vs 4,000: HR 0.49 (95% CI 0.44 to 0.55). 12,000 steps: HR 0.35 (0.28 to 0.45). 4,840 adults aged 40+, 1,165 deaths, mean follow-up 10.1 years. Step intensity added nothing once total steps were counted.
- Citation: Saint-Maurice PF, et al. Association of Daily Step Count and Step Intensity With Mortality Among US Adults. JAMA. 2020;323(12):1151-1160. doi:10.1001/jama.2020.1382
- URL: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2763292
- Confidence: High. **Opened.** Observational, so say "associated with".

**Fact 2 (steps, pooled)**
- Print: "Across 47,471 adults in 15 studies, the most active quarter of walkers had about half the death risk of the least active quarter."
- Stat: Median steps/day by quartile: 3,553, 5,801, 7,842, 10,901. HR for all-cause death vs lowest quartile: 0.60 (Q2), 0.55 (Q3), 0.47 (Q4, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.57). 3,013 deaths, median follow-up 7.1 years. Benefit plateau by age (about 6,000 to 8,000 steps if 60+; 8,000 to 10,000 if under 60): **Secondary** only.
- Citation: Paluch AE, et al. Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. Lancet Public Health. 2022;7(3):e219-e228. doi:10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9
- Confidence: Medium. Quartile figures from a search excerpt (abstract blocked).

**Fact 3 (small doses)**
- Print: "About 15 minutes of activity a day was linked to a 14% lower risk of death and about three extra years of life expectancy, in a study of 416,175 Taiwanese adults."
- Stat: Low group (about 92 min/week) vs inactive: HR 0.86 (95% CI 0.81 to 0.91). Each extra 15 min/day: a further 4% lower all-cause mortality.
- Citation: Wen CP, et al. Minimum amount of physical activity for reduced mortality and extended life expectancy: a prospective cohort study. Lancet. 2011;378(9798):1244-1253.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21846575/
- Confidence: Medium-high. Figures with CIs came from a search excerpt. Activity was self-reported.

**Fact 4 (chronic disease burden)**
- Print: "Physical inactivity is estimated to cause 9% of early deaths worldwide, and about 7% of type 2 diabetes and 6% of coronary heart disease."
- Stat: 9% (range 5.1 to 12.5) of premature mortality, over 5.3 million deaths in 2008. Population attributable fractions: 6% of coronary heart disease, 7% of type 2 diabetes, 10% of breast cancer, 10% of colon cancer.
- Citation: Lee IM, et al. Effect of physical inactivity on major non-communicable diseases worldwide. Lancet. 2012;380(9838):219-229.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22818936/
- Confidence: Medium-high. Figures from a search excerpt. These are population estimates, so keep "estimated".

**Fact 5 (cancer)**
- Print: "Higher leisure-time activity was linked to a lower risk of 13 types of cancer."
- Stat: 1.44 million adults, 12 cohorts, 26 cancer types. 13 types showed lower risk (hazard ratios about 0.58 for esophageal adenocarcinoma to 0.90 for breast).
- Citation: Moore SC, et al. Association of leisure-time physical activity with risk of 26 types of cancer in 1.44 million adults. JAMA Internal Medicine. 2016.
- URL: https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/increased-physical-activity-associated-lower-risk-13-types-cancer
- Confidence: Medium. NIH release and search excerpt; the paper itself was not opened. Volume and pages not verified.

**Fact 6 (depression, network meta-analysis)**
- Print: "Exercise is an effective treatment for depression, and walking or jogging, yoga and strength training were among the most effective types."
- Stat: 218 studies, 495 arms, 14,170 participants. Compared exercise with psychotherapy, antidepressants and control.
- Citation: Noetel M, et al. Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. 2024;384:e075847. doi:10.1136/bmj-2023-075847
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847
- Confidence: Medium. Abstract blocked (403). Do not quote effect sizes: the BMJ issued a correction in May 2024 on how they were calculated.

**Fact 7 (depression, umbrella review)**
- Print: "Across 97 reviews and 128,000 participants, physical activity was linked to less depression, anxiety and distress than usual care."
- Stat: Median effect sizes vs usual care: depression -0.43, anxiety -0.42, psychological distress -0.60 (95% CI -0.78 to -0.42, from one review of six trials, so less robust). 1,039 trials in total.
- Citation: Singh B, Olds T, Curtis R, et al. Effectiveness of physical activity interventions for improving depression, anxiety and distress: an overview of systematic reviews. Br J Sports Med. 2023;57(18):1203-1209. doi:10.1136/bjsports-2022-106195
- URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10579187/
- Confidence: High. **Opened.** Do not use the press claim that exercise works "1.5 times better" than medication: I could not verify it.

**Fact 8 (cognition)**
- Print: "In a one-year trial, older adults who walked about 40 minutes a day, three days a week, grew a memory-related brain area by about 2%."
- Stat: 120 sedentary older adults. Anterior hippocampus volume rose about 2%, offsetting 1 to 2 years of age-related loss. Control group did stretching and toning.
- Citation: Erickson KI, et al. Exercise training increases size of hippocampus and improves memory. PNAS. 2011.
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21282661/
- Confidence: Medium. Abstract text from search excerpt. One summary site said 7 weeks; the original trial was one year.

**Fact 9 (short bursts)**
- Print: "Short bursts of vigorous activity in everyday life, such as one to two minutes of fast stair climbing, were linked to lower mortality."
- Stat: 25,241 UK Biobank adults who did no exercise, about 6.9 years. Median 3 bouts/day vs none: roughly 38% to 40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality, 48% to 49% lower cardiovascular mortality. Median 4.4 min/day: 26% to 30% lower all-cause mortality.
- Citation: Stamatakis E, et al. Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality. Nature Medicine. 2022. doi:10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x
- Confidence: Medium. Secondary summary; observational. Exercise snacks as an intervention remain unproven.

**Fact 10 (weekend warriors)**
- Print: "People who fit their activity into one or two sessions a week still had lower death rates than inactive people."
- Stat: About 30% lower all-cause mortality vs inactive (press figure). Hazard ratios: 0.53 cardiovascular mortality, 0.70 cancer mortality. About 63,600 adults over 40 in England and Scotland.
- Citation: O'Donovan G, et al. Association of "weekend warrior" and other leisure time physical activity patterns with risks for all-cause, cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine. 2017;177(3):335-342. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.8014
- URL: https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.8014
- Confidence: Low-medium. 30% is from press coverage; paper not opened. Year is 2017, not 2022.

## 3. Habit formation

- **Duration.** Lally P, et al. How are habits formed: modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 2010. doi:10.1002/ejsp.674. 96 people repeated one daily behaviour for 84 days. Average time to maximum automaticity: 66 days (range 18 to 254 days, upper figure extrapolated). A single missed day had little effect. Print: "On average about two months, but it varies widely from person to person." Confidence: medium-high (**Opened** via BPS summary). Some secondary sources call 66 days a median; BPS calls it an average.
- **Implementation intentions.** Gollwitzer PM, Sheeran P. Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 2006;38:69-119. doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1. Average effect d = 0.65 across 94 independent tests (**Secondary**). Print: "Deciding in advance when and where you will act, such as 'after breakfast I will walk for 10 minutes', makes you more likely to follow through."
- **Caution.** The often-quoted 91% vs 35% exercise result (Milne, Orbell and Sheeran, 2002) is not a universal rate (**Secondary**). I found no trial of habit stacking or starting small: present them as tips, not findings.

## 4. What counts as moderate vs vigorous (MET conversion)

Intensity bands (US convention, **Secondary**): light 1.1 to 2.9 METs; moderate 3.0 to 5.9 METs; vigorous 6.0 METs or more.

| Activity | Intensity | METs | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Walking about 3.0 mph (4.8 km/h) | Moderate | 3.5 | Ainsworth 2011 (**Secondary**) |
| Walking about 3.5 mph (5.6 km/h) | Moderate | 4.3 | Ainsworth 2011 (**Secondary**) |
| Running 6 mph (9.7 km/h) | Vigorous | 9.8 | Ainsworth 2011 (**Secondary**) |
| Cycling, swimming, strength work | Not verified | n/a | https://pacompendium.com |

## 5. Gaps and cautions

- Not verified: Paluch age-specific plateau, Noetel effect sizes, heart disease and diabetes dose-response figures, full 2018 HHS text.
- Do not print the "1.5 times better than medication" claim.
- Most facts are observational: use "linked to" or "associated with", not "causes".
